### [Researcher&\#x27;s 2 Million Canaries Expose AI Supply-Chain Data Leaks](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/11/a-leaky-ai-supply-chain-is-exposing-secret-user-data-at-an-alarming-rate/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Security researcher HD Moore planted 2 million unique canaries—previously unused AWS access keys, SSH passwords, URLs, DNS names, and private git repository references—into individual prompts sent through AI routing and gateway services, according to an Ars Technica report. He controlled the infrastructure tied to those secrets so he could detect any later use and identify which link in the AI supply chain had leaked them. The experiment found that services such as OpenRouter and Vercel, which broker prompts to inference providers, exposed highly sensitive data. The supplied excerpt does not detail the leak mechanics or quantify the leakage rate, so the findings are reported only at a high level.

rss · Ars Technica · Nov 20, 20:59

**「What an AI router does」** Many AI applications do not call Anthropic, OpenAI, or another model provider directly. Instead they send end-user prompts to an &quot;AI router&quot; or &quot;AI gateway&quot; such as OpenRouter or Vercel, which forwards each request to whichever inference engine is cheapest or best suited to the task. That brokering step means prompts pass through intermediaries the app developer does not operate, which is the surface HD Moore&\#x27;s canary experiment was designed to test.

**「Impact」** Developers who route prompts through AI routers or gateways cannot assume Zero Data Retention settings are honored: in the same experiment, three providers were breached, prompts were stolen, and retention settings were ignored, per Dragos&\#x27;s account of the findings. Teams that pass credentials, internal URLs, or customer data inside prompts should treat the router as part of their trust boundary and test what leaves it rather than relying on contractual retention terms.

### [Paper claims language models can rewrite their own weights without training](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21885) ⭐️ 7.0/10

A new arXiv paper claims that a language model can rewrite its own weights through its own reasoning and improve without gradients or training; a quoted passage says its accuracy rose across eight consecutive self-editing sessions by testing candidate edits on practice problems and keeping only those that work. The supplied material does not identify the model, benchmarks, or an independent replication, so the magnitude and generality of the claimed gain remain unverified here.

hackernews · lwhsiao · Nov 20, 16:29 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50577265)

**「Background」** Prior work has already shown that models can modify their own weights or generate their own update data: MIT&\#x27;s SEAL framework let a model generate fine-tuning data and update directives \(tool-2-3\), and a separate weight-repair effort used a GPT-6 Astra agent to correct Qwen3.5-0.8B&\#x27;s reasoning loops without gradients across 158 agent turns \(tool-2-2\). The new claim is that a model can rewrite its weights directly from its own reasoning with no gradients and no training, so the key question is whether this produces gains beyond fixing an obvious flaw such as overlong reasoning traces.

**「Impact」** If the claimed capability holds, weight-level self-modification becomes part of the deployment threat model: safety reviewers would need to check whether a model’s own edits can suppress safety-relevant activations, a risk commenters raised for autonomous self-editing.

**「Community discussion」** Hacker News commenters disputed the “no training” framing: aesthesia argued that the only self-modification described was shortening reasoning traces to avoid a token limit, and Toutouxc said the test-edits-and-keep-what-works loop sounds like training. gavinray cited Google’s SLEEP framework as prior work on self-updating weights, while LarsDu88 warned that an autonomous model could direct itself to suppress safety-related activations.

### [Why Caching LLM Outputs Barely Pays Off](https://seangoedecke.com/why-output-caching-doesnt-work/) ⭐️ 6.0/10

rss · Sean Goedecke · Nov 21, 00:00

**「Background」** LLM inference economics already depends on caching inputs in the KV cache so the full conversation history is not recomputed on every message. The author asks whether outputs deserve similar treatment, since agents often repeat code snippets, evidence, or sample outputs, and output tokens are much more expensive to generate than input tokens are to ingest.

**「Solution」** The proposed mechanism is to fine-tune or prompt models to emit references such as file-and-line ranges or pointers into conversation history instead of regurgitating content, with the harness transparently expanding those references for the user. But the author argues this optimizes only visible output: he suspects models rarely repeat themselves in internal chain of thought because that information is already available to them, though he is only moderately confident. From his own agent histories, he estimates about 50% of output tokens are reasoning tokens, 44% are tool calls, and just 6% are visible output. Amdahl&\#x27;s law then caps the payoff: even saving two-thirds of visible output, an implausibly optimistic figure, would cut only about 4% of total output tokens. The real target is the invisible 94%, which depends largely on model intelligence; smarter models may need fewer reasoning and tool-call tokens, so a model charging 5x more per token but using 6x fewer tokens can cost less overall. The author acknowledges that the 6% estimate is anecdotal and that tool-call repetition remains an open question.

**「Takeaway」** The author concludes that output caching is unlikely to pay off because it targets only a small visible fraction of agent output, while the larger opportunity lies in reducing invisible reasoning and tool-call tokens through greater model intelligence.
